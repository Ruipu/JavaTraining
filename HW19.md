## Team Setup

1. **How many people are on your team and why?**

My team has 10 people. That's enough to cover every role we need — product, design, engineering, QA, and DevOps — without getting too big to coordinate. Anything larger and communication starts to break down.

**2. List every team member — name, title, and one-line responsibility**

- Sarah Chen — Product Owner — owns the backlog and represents stakeholder needs
- Marcus Webb — Scrum Master — runs all scrum ceremonies and removes blockers
- Mia Russo — UX Designer — designs mockups in Figma before dev begins
- Priya Nair — QA Tester — writes test cases and tracks bugs in Jira
- James Torres — DevOps Engineer — manages CI/CD pipeline and AWS infrastructure
- Amy Liu — Business Analyst — writes user stories and bridges business and engineering
- Derek Okonkwo — Tech Lead — makes architecture decisions and reviews code
- Kevin Zhao — Full Stack Developer — builds frontend and backend features
- Lisa Park — Full Stack Developer — builds frontend and backend features
- Simon Gao — Backend Developer — builds REST APIs and manages the database

**3. Who is your Product Owner and what do they do day to day?**

My Product Owner is Sarah Chen. Every day she prioritizes the backlog, writes and refines user stories, and answers questions from the dev team about requirements. She also communicates with stakeholders and makes sure the team is building the right things.

**4. Who is your Scrum Master and how are they different from a project manager?**

My Scrum Master is Marcus Webb. The difference is that a project manager tells people what to do and tracks deadlines. Marcus doesn't manage anyone. His job is to protect the team, run the ceremonies, and remove anything that's blocking us from moving forward.

**5. How many engineers do you have and how are they split?**

My team has 4 engineers. One backend, two full stack, and one tech lead who also codes. We don't have a dedicated frontend engineer because our full stack developers handle both sides.

**6. Do you have a QA engineer? Is testing their job alone?**

Yes, we have Priya Nair as our QA Tester. But testing is not her job alone. Every developer is responsible for writing unit tests for their own code. Priya handles integration testing, regression testing, and bug tracking. The whole team owns quality.

**7. Who handles deployments and infrastructure on your team?**

James Torres is our DevOps Engineer. He owns the CI/CD pipeline and our AWS infrastructure. When code gets merged, James's pipeline automatically builds, tests, and deploys it.

**8. Do you have a designer? Where do they fit in the sprint cycle?**

Yes, Mia Russo is our UX Designer. She works one sprint ahead of the developers. While the team is building sprint 1 features, Mia is already designing the mockups for sprint 2. That way developers always have assets ready when they need them.

## How your team works?

**1. How long are your sprints and why did you choose that length?**

My team runs two-week sprints. One week is too short to finish anything meaningful, and four weeks is too long to catch problems early. Two weeks gives us enough time to deliver real features while still being able to adjust direction every cycle.

**2. Walk me through what happens during Sprint Planning.**

At the start of each sprint, the whole team gets together for sprint planning. Sarah presents the top items from the backlog. Derek and the developers estimate the effort for each ticket using story points. We then decide how many tickets we can realistically finish in two weeks based on our velocity. By the end of the meeting, every ticket in the sprint has an owner and everyone knows what they are building.

**3. What does your Daily Standup look like — format, duration, who runs it?**

Our standup is every morning at 10am on Zoom. It runs 15 minutes maximum. Marcus runs it. Each person answers three questions: what did I finish yesterday, what am I working on today, and do I have any blockers. If something needs more discussion, Marcus takes it offline after standup.

**4. What is a Sprint Review and who attends yours?**

A Sprint Review is at the end of every sprint. The team demos what they built to stakeholders. Sarah attends, the developers demo their own work, and stakeholders give feedback. It's not a presentation — it's a live demo of working software.

**5. What is a Retrospective and what does your team actually do in it?**

A Retrospective happens right after the Sprint Review. It's just the team, no stakeholders. We go through three things: what went well, what didn't go well, and what we will do differently next sprint. Marcus facilitates it. We always leave with at least one concrete action item.

**6. What is Backlog Refinement and when does your team do it?**

Backlog Refinement is when we review and clean up upcoming tickets before they go into a sprint. Sarah adds new user stories, the team asks clarifying questions, and Derek breaks down anything that's too large. We do it every Wednesday in the middle of the sprint so we're always ready for the next planning session.

**7. What is your team's Definition of Done?**

A ticket is done when the code is written, unit tests pass, Priya has tested it, it has been reviewed and approved by Derek, and it is deployed to the staging environment. If any of those steps are missing, the ticket is not done.

## Communication & Escalation

**1. You found a critical bug two days before release — what do you do and who do you tell?**

I file a bug ticket in Jira immediately and mark it as critical. Then I tell Marcus and Sarah right away. Sarah decides whether we delay the release or ship without that feature. I don't try to fix it quietly on my own — I surface it immediately so the right people can make that call.

**2. A stakeholder wants to add a feature mid-sprint — how does your team handle it?**

We don't add it to the current sprint. Sarah logs it in the backlog and it gets prioritized for the next sprint planning session. The only exception is if it's a critical bug or a legal issue. Otherwise the sprint stays protected.

**3. You are blocked on a ticket and cannot finish before the sprint ends — who do you talk to?**

I bring it up in standup the moment I know I'm blocked. Marcus helps remove the blocker. If it's a technical problem, I go to Derek. If the sprint is ending and the ticket won't make it, I tell Marcus so we can update the sprint board and plan accordingly.

**4. Two engineers disagree on a technical approach — how is that resolved on your team?**

Both engineers present their approach to Derek. He makes the final call. If it's a bigger architectural decision, we have a quick team discussion, but Derek has the final say as Tech Lead. We don't let disagreements sit — we resolve them fast and move on.

**5. The designer is out sick and you need assets to move forward — what do you do?**

Because Mia works one sprint ahead, we should already have her designs ready. If for some reason we don't, I use her existing Figma files as reference and build what I can. I flag it to Marcus so the team knows there may be visual adjustments needed when she returns.

**6. Your deployment pipeline breaks and blocks the whole team — who owns that problem?**

James Torres owns it immediately. He drops everything else and fixes it. Marcus flags it in our team Slack channel so everyone knows why deployments are paused. If James needs help, Derek jumps in. Pipeline issues are the highest priority because they block the entire team.

## Documentation & Artifacts

**1. What is a Product Backlog and who owns it on your team?**

The Product Backlog is the full list of everything we want to build — every feature, bug fix, and improvement. Sarah Chen owns it. She decides what's in it, what order things are in, and what gets worked on next.

**2. What is a Sprint Backlog and how does it get created?**

The Sprint Backlog is the list of tickets we commit to finishing in one sprint. It gets created during Sprint Planning. Sarah presents the top backlog items, the team estimates them, and we pull in as many as we can realistically finish in two weeks.

**3. When does your team write a technical design doc and who has to approve it?**

We write a technical design doc for any feature that touches multiple services, changes the database schema, or introduces a new architectural pattern. Derek writes it or reviews it, and Sarah has to approve it from a product perspective before we start building.

**4. How does your team file and track bugs?**

Priya files all bugs in Jira with steps to reproduce, expected behavior, and actual behavior. Each bug gets a severity label — critical, high, medium, or low. Critical bugs go straight to the top of the backlog. Everything else gets prioritized by Sarah in the next refinement session.

**5. Where does your team write things down — what tools do you use and why?**

We use Jira for tickets and sprint tracking, Confluence for documentation and design docs, Figma for UI designs, and Slack for daily communication. We use these tools because they integrate well together and the whole team can access everything in one place.

## Bigger Picture

**1. How does your team decide what goes into the next sprint?**

During Backlog Refinement and Sprint Planning, Sarah presents the highest priority items. The team estimates effort and we pull in tickets based on our velocity from the previous sprint. User value and business priority drive the order, not what's easiest to build.

**2. What happens if your team consistently doesn't finish sprint work?**

We bring it up in the Retrospective. Usually it means we're overcommitting — our estimates are too optimistic. Derek and Marcus look at our velocity over the last few sprints and we adjust how much we pull in. If the same person is always missing tickets, Marcus has a private conversation with them to understand what's blocking them.

**3. How does a brand new feature go from idea to production on your team?**

A stakeholder or Sarah has an idea. Amy writes it up as a user story. Sarah adds it to the backlog and prioritizes it. During refinement, Derek and the team break it into tickets and estimate them. It gets pulled into a sprint during planning. Mia designs it first, then developers build it, Priya tests it, James deploys it to staging, and after Sarah approves it in the Sprint Review, James promotes it to production.

**4. If someone asked you in an interview "describe your team" — what would you say in 60 seconds?**

My team has 10 people building StudySync, a mobile app that helps college students find study groups. We run two-week sprints. Sarah Chen is our Product Owner — she owns the roadmap and the backlog. Marcus Webb is our Scrum Master — he keeps us on process and removes blockers. We have a Tech Lead, two full stack developers, one backend developer, a QA tester, a DevOps engineer, a UX designer, and a business analyst. Everyone has a clear role but we all own quality together. Our pipeline is fully automated, our designer works one sprint ahead, and we ship working software at the end of every sprint.

## Part 2

**At 9:55 AM** 

I open Slack. Marcus has already posted the standup reminder in our team channel. I pull up Jira to check where I left off yesterday. I was halfway through the API endpoint for study group matching, and I had one comment from Derek on my pull request that I hadn't addressed yet.

**At 10:00 AM** 

We jump on Zoom. All ten of us are there. Marcus starts the timer. We go around the room fast. Sarah says she finished reviewing the user stories for sprint 2 and will have them ready for Wednesday refinement. Mia says she pushed the new mockups for the group chat screen to Figma. Amy flags that a stakeholder asked about adding a notifications feature, and Sarah immediately says it goes in the backlog, not this sprint. Marcus nods and moves on.

When it's my turn I say: I finished the group creation endpoint yesterday, today I'm working on the matching algorithm, and I have one blocker. I'm not sure how we want to handle the case where a student's schedule has no overlap with any existing group. Do we create a new group automatically or return an empty result? Derek says let's talk after standup. Marcus closes the meeting at 10:14 AM.

**Derek and I jump into a separate Zoom room.** 

He pulls up the technical design doc in Confluence and we look at the original spec together. We decide that if there's no match, the API should return an empty list and the frontend will prompt the user to create a new group. Simple. I update the doc and get back to my code.

**By noon** 

I have the core matching logic working. I write my unit tests, push the branch to GitHub, and open a pull request. I tag Derek as reviewer and drop a message in our Slack dev channel so he knows it's ready.

**At 1:30 PM** 

Priya sends me a message. She found a bug in the authentication flow: Users are getting logged out unexpectedly after switching screens. She already filed it in Jira as SCRUM-14, marked high severity. I check the ticket, reproduce the issue locally in about ten minutes, and find that the JWT token expiration logic has an off-by-one error. It's a small fix but an important one. I push a hotfix branch and tell Priya it's ready for her to retest.

**At 2:00 PM** 

I get a Slack message from James. He says the staging deployment from this morning finished successfully and the new group creation endpoint is live on staging. He pastes the environment URL and I do a quick smoke test. Everything looks good.

**Around 3:00 PM** 

I get Derek's review back on my matching algorithm pull request. He has two comments. One is a suggestion about query optimization, the other is a question about edge cases with students who have no courses listed. I fix the optimization, add a guard clause for the edge case, and reply to his comments. He approves it fifteen minutes later and I merge it.

**By 4:30 PM** 

Priya confirms the authentication bug is fixed on her end. She closes SCRUM-14 in Jira and moves it to Done. I move my matching algorithm ticket to In Review and update the sprint board.

At 5:00 PM I write a quick end-of-day note in our team Slack channel, which is about what I finished, what's in review, and what I'm picking up tomorrow. Marcus reads it and gives it a thumbs up. That's the signal that the day is done.

*It was not a dramatic day. No crisis, no all-hands emergency. Just standup, code, a blocker resolved in fifteen minutes, a bug caught and fixed before it reached production, and two tickets moved forward on the board. That's what a good day on this team looks like.*
